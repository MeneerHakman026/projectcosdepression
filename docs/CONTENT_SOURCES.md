## 1. COS-D current project

*Source for this entire section: Veal 2026, unless noted otherwise.*

### What COS-D is

COS-D is a research project to develop a **Core Outcome Set (COS) for adults with depression** — an agreed, standardised minimum set of outcomes that should be measured in every clinical trial (and other study design) evaluating treatment for depression (Veal 2026, p.2).

A COS has two parts (Veal 2026, p.2, citing the COMET Handbook):
- **What to measure** — the *outcome domains* (e.g. mood, social isolation, ability to work)
- **How to measure it** — the *outcome measures/instruments* (e.g. a specific questionnaire or scale)

COS-D is being developed for use in **clinical research** specifically (trials and other efficacy studies), as distinct from outcome sets intended for routine clinical practice, such as the existing ICHOM depression/anxiety set (Veal 2026, p.2).

*Plain-English framing suitable for the website:* "COS-D is working out which outcomes researchers should always measure in depression studies — the things that actually matter to people living with depression — so that different studies can be fairly compared."

### Why it is being developed

Two problems motivate COS-D, both stated directly in Veal 2026 (p.2):

1. **The wrong things are measured.** Outcome domains currently used in depression trials do not fully represent what matters to people with lived experience. In the PROCEED study, 80 outcome domains were identified as mattering to patients, carers, and clinicians — but *"less than a quarter of these outcome domains were measured in late-stage RCTs of depression"* (Veal 2026, p.2, citing Veal 2024).
2. **Different studies measure things differently.** Outcome *measures* are heterogeneous across depression trials — *"at most 40% of recent and ongoing RCTs using a common measure"* (Veal 2026, p.2, citing Veal 2024). This heterogeneity limits the ability to compare or combine (meta-analyse) trial results, which is a source of research waste (Veal 2026, p.2; Controversy/Debate 2020, p.207).

*Plain-English framing:* "Depression trials often measure different things, in different ways — and frequently miss what patients themselves say matters most. That makes it hard to know which treatments really work, or to combine results across studies."

### Target population

The COS will cover (Veal 2026, p.3, "Scope of the Core Outcome Set"):

- **Adults** (over 18 years old) with depression
- **No exclusion by comorbidity** — physical or psychiatric comorbidities are included
- The **diverse experiences** of depression: isolated episodes, recurrent (depressive disorder or bipolar disorder), and chronic/persistent depression
- **Any intervention type** — psychotherapies, drugs, neurostimulation, physical activity, complementary and alternative medicines
- **Any study design** evaluating treatment efficacy, including RCTs and non-randomised studies (with an eye to future use in emulated target trials and routine/electronic health record data) (Veal 2026, p.3, p.9)

### Stakeholder groups

Three key interest-holder groups participate throughout COS-D development (Veal 2026, p.4, "2.3.2.2. Population"):

1. **People with lived experience of depression** — self-reported history or ongoing experience of depression
2. **Health care professionals** — self-reported professional experience with people with depression (e.g. GPs, psychiatrists, psychologists, nurses)
3. **Carers** (referred to as "informal caregivers" in the earlier PROCEED paper) — self-reported history or ongoing experience of supporting a person with depression (e.g. family, partner, housemate)

In addition, a **steering committee of 14 key interest-holders**, three of whom are people with lived experience of depression, oversees the design and development of the COS and are co-authors of the protocol (Veal 2026, p.2).

*Note on terminology:* Chevance 2020 uses "informal caregivers"; Veal 2026 uses "carers" for the same stakeholder group. The website (Dictionary/About) should pick one term consistently — **flagged for confirmation from Chris.**

### Development methodology

COS-D follows a **three-step approach**, adapted from the COMET Handbook and from the methodological reforms proposed in Controversy/Debate 2020 (Veal 2026, p.3, Figure 1):

**Step 1 — Generation of outcome domains** *(complete)*
- Method: large international online survey with open-ended questions about the expected benefit of treatment
- This step **is** the PROCEED study (Chevance 2020), published in 2020
- Result: 80 candidate outcome domains (64 symptom-related, 16 functioning-related), described in a "Depression Dictionary"

**Step 2 — Selection of core outcome domains** *(protocol published; not yet conducted — no results exist)*
- Method: a large online international **preference elicitation survey**, followed by a consensus meeting
- Participants rate/rank the 80 candidate domains via one of three possible task designs (approval voting + Likert rating; ranking pyramid; randomised subset ranking) — the final task design will be chosen after piloting
- Target sample (recruitment **goals**, not yet achieved): **2000** people with lived experience of depression, **500** health care professionals, **300** carers (Veal 2026, p.4)
- Survey to be offered in **five languages**: English, French, German, Italian, Spanish, targeting **France, Germany, Spain, Italy, and the UK** (Veal 2026, p.4–5)
- A consensus meeting with 15–20 key interest-holder representatives will then decide the final core outcome domain set, using a nominal group technique (Veal 2026, p.6)

**Step 3 — Selection of core outcome measures** *(planned, not yet conducted)*
- Method: a COSMIN (COnsensus-based Standards for the selection of health Measurement INstruments) review of outcome measures already frequently used in depression RCTs, matched against the core outcome domains for quality and content coverage
- A further consensus meeting will select the final core outcome measurement set (Veal 2026, p.7–8)

## 2. Research background

### PROCEED study (Chevance 2020)

An international online survey (French, German, English) run April–December 2018, asking open-ended questions about expected treatment benefits, to identify which outcome domains matter to people living with depression.

- **3003 participants** completed at least one open-ended question: **1912 patients**, **464 informal caregivers**, **627 health-care professionals**, from **52 countries** (Chevance 2020, p.695)
- **8183 open-ended answers** were analysed using inductive qualitative content analysis (Chevance 2020, p.695)
- Identified **137 outcome domains** in total: **80 domains** regarding the benefits of treatment (64 symptom domains + 16 functioning domains), and **57 other domains** covering treatment safety, healthcare organisation, and social representation of depression (Chevance 2020, p.695, Figure 1)
- Data saturation models confirmed that at least 90% of all possible domains had been identified for every participant group (Chevance 2020, p.695)
- The paper explicitly frames itself as **the first step** toward a COS for depression, not the COS itself: *"Results from this study should set the foundation for a core outcome set for depression"* (Chevance 2020, Summary/Interpretation, p.692)

An accompanying independent commentary (Commentary 2020) praised the participatory approach but noted limitations worth keeping in mind for the website's framing of PROCEED as background: the sample skewed toward high-income countries and France specifically (1417/1912, 74% of patients were French), and toward women (75% of patients); the commentary calls for future research to reach more diverse voices (Commentary 2020, p.651).

### Earlier methodological work on Core Outcome Sets

Controversy/Debate 2020 is a methods paper (not a study with new participant data) that diagnoses weaknesses in standard COS-development practice and proposes the reforms that COS-D Step 2 and Step 3 now implement:

- Standard COS methods (as recommended by COMET/OMERACT) typically generate outcome domains via a **systematic review plus small qualitative studies** (focus groups/interviews with 5–15 patients, rarely international) (Controversy/Debate 2020, p.207–209, Figure 1)
- Only **20%** of published COS included patients in their development at all; of those, **57%** involved patients from a single country only (Controversy/Debate 2020, p.207)
- Standard COS *selection* of outcomes typically uses a **Delphi method**, which the authors argue is problematic for mixing patients ("experts of lived experience") with professional experts, has high attrition between rounds, and requires "COS literacy" that acts as a barrier to patient participation (Controversy/Debate 2020, p.210)
- The paper proposes instead: (1) generating domains via large international **online surveys with open-ended questions** rather than focus groups; (2) **separating preference elicitation from decision-making** — a large-scale online preference-ranking survey to gather individual preferences, followed by a smaller facilitated consensus meeting; (3) involving patients directly in selecting outcome **measurement instruments**, not just domains (Controversy/Debate 2020, p.209–210)
- As proof of concept, the authors cite PROCEED's recruitment speed: **1000 participants after 1 week, 2000 after 2 months** (following social media/professional mailing-list advertisement), **3000 after another 4 months** through snowball effect alone (Controversy/Debate 2020, p.211)

This paper is the direct methodological blueprint for COS-D's Step 2 (preference elicitation survey + consensus meeting) and Step 3 (patient involvement in measure selection) as described in Veal 2026.

### Evidence about heterogeneity of outcome measures in depression trials

Veal 2024 is a systematic review quantifying the outcome-measurement heterogeneity problem that motivates COS-D, and separately checking how well existing measures actually cover the 80 PROCEED domains.

- Reviewed **450 RCTs** of adult unipolar or bipolar depression, registered or with protocols published **2018–2022** (Veal 2024, p.286, Figure 1)
- Identified **388 different outcome measures** in use across those 450 trials (Veal 2024, p.290)
- **40%** of the 450 RCTs used the same single measure — the **Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD)** (Veal 2024, p.285, Summary)
- Matched the content of the **25 most frequently used** measures (10 patient-reported, 9 clinician-reported, 6 performance-based) against the 80 PROCEED domains, using patients and clinicians to do the matching (Veal 2024, p.288)
- The **HAMD**, the single most-used measure, covers a **maximum of 47 of 80 domains (59%)** — the highest coverage of any measure reviewed (Veal 2024, p.285, Summary; p.291)
- **Seven of the 80 domains (9%)** that matter to patients were **not covered by any** of the 25 most-used measures — including **mental pain** and **irritability** (Veal 2024, p.285, Summary)
- The most commonly used *patient-reported* measures (PROMs) in depression trials were often originally developed as **screening tools**, not designed to track treatment progress (e.g. PHQ-9, EPDS) — raising validity questions for their use in trials (Veal 2024, p.291, Discussion)
